Catfishing Basics

What is Catfishing?

Catfishing is the practice of fishing for catfish. Catfish are a popular bottom-feeding freshwater fish, but they can also live in saltwater. Catfishing is done for both sport and food. They live in all types of bodies of water including rivers, lakes, and ponds. Freshwater catfish species include:

  • Blue Catfish
  • Flathead Catfish
  • Channel Catfish
  • Black Bullhead
  • Yellow Bullhead
  • White Catfish
  • Pleco (invasive species)
While most catfish reside in freshwater, there are a few saltwater species, the most common being the Hardhead Catfish and the Gafftopsail Catfish. Both of which are native to the Gulf of Mexico.

How to Catfish

In order to catfish, you must have specific equipment. This includes, rod, reel, line, hooks, bobbers, weights, swivels, etc. Catfish come in all shapes and sizes making it somewhat difficult to target a specific species. You can catch catfish on a variety of different weights of rod and reel, but the tackle setup is generally the same.

The Rod

For catfishing, you generally want to have a heavier rod with a slower action. For example, a 7 foot, medium-heavy, slow rod. This is to take into account for how large catfish can get, with some blue catfish exceeding 100 pounds. In accordance with the rod, you will need a stronger reel with a higher drag weight. This will allow you to more easily reel in the fish once you have a bite. Catfish are extremely strong predators and can easily break a lighter rod. Since they are so strong, and can easily weigh 50 pounds, you will require stronger fishing line. I recommend 50-100 pound braided line with a 5 foot fluorocarbon leader.

The Tackle

Catfishing requires more tackle than the average fish. The reason behind this is that they are bottom-feeders. They tend to stick to the bottom of the body of water where the water is cool. This allows them to conserve energy to use to strike on baitfish. In order to increase your hookups with catfish, you can use several different specialized rigs:

  • Carolina
  • Santee-Copper
  • Three-Way
  • Dragging
  • Slip Float
  • Double-hook
  • Dropper
  • Float-and-Fly
All of these rigs do different things in the water column, and are used in different settings and environments. To determine which is the best for your conditions, examine the water and bottom. Ask "are there snags?" and "how deep is the water?" to help decide which rig to use.

You will need several different pieces of tackle in order to effectively catfish. You will need, weights of varying size, 3/0-8/0 circle hooks, peg floats, swivels, beads, float stops, and leader line. All of this tackle can come together in hundreds of different ways to give you the best catfish rig. All of the aforementioned rigs can be tied using the tackle mentioned in this paragraph.

Hooks

The hooks are arguably the most important part of the tackle. Without the hooks, you simply cannot catch the fish. However, you cannot just use any hook in the fishing section of your local outdoor store. You need specific hooks called circle hooks. These hooks set themselves upon the fish grabbing the bait and swimming away. It sets it nicely in the corner of the mouth and easily removes with the help of its circular shape. If using live or cut bait, use 5/0-8/0 circle hooks for best chance at hookups.

Weights

Weights are an equally important part of catfishing. Picking weights makes the difference between catching fish and catching snags. When picking weight sizes and shapes, it is important to consider what type of rig you are going to be putting them on. For a lot of catfishing rigs, sliding egg weights are the superior option, with the weight being entirely dependent on your setup. You want a weight that will take your bait to the bottom without getting stuck, and one that will relay taps through the rod.

The Bait

When catfishing, you can use an extremely large variety of baits. Catfish have very poor vision, this allows them to have exemplary smell and touch. They have these whisker-like barbels that are extremely sensitive and help them detect vibrations and chemicals in the water. This, along with their sense of smell and nocturnal activity makes them ferocious predators. We can use this knowledge to our advantage to know what they will eat.

Live Bait

Live bait is probably the most efficient and effective bait for catfish. The smell of the blood from the bait and well as the bait moving and thrashing around sends vibrations through the water that catfish can detect. Live bait is a flathead catfish's favorite snack, and is most effective on them. Channel catfish will also eat live bait but, they tend to stick to smaller baits like worms. Live bait can be anything from a bluegill to tiny worms, but they flatheads do prefer large bluegill.

Cut Bait

Cut bait is the most versatile of the three bait options as you can use any non-game fish or any raw meat as bait. Many anglers swear by using hotdogs and chicken livers as bait for channel catfish. When discussing cut bait, it is important to know that blue catfish prefer cut bait the most. Their favorite cut baits are shad and skipjacks, while cut bluegill and other panfish also work well. Catfish like the cut bait because they can smell the bait through the water since it is cut. It allows the bait to bleed into the water and essentially chum it to attract fish.

Artificial Bait

Artificial bait is when you take everything you know about how and what catfish eat and throw it all on the hook together. There are hundreds of different kinds of artificial baits you can buy, so this page will not cover all of them. The main artificial baits used are:

  • Scents
  • Doughs or Punches
  • Dyes
While all of these are excellent, the only ones you can really use by itself are the doughs and punch baits. For these, you simply dip your hook down into the bait, twist it, and pull it out to see a glob of dough. A proven combination of artificial and cut bait is chopping up hotdogs, putting them in a ziploc, and pouring powder Kool-Aid over them. You then shake the bag and let the powder dissolve. This creates a pungent scent that catfish love. On top of that, you could even add some garlic or fish oil to really bring out the scent. Marshmallows are also a proven good choice for catfish bait.

Where to Cast

Finally, you have picked your rod and reel, tied on your rig, and baited your hook. You are now ready to fish. You may be asking yourself, "but where do I fish?," "where do I cast?," and those are excellent questions. To decide where to fish, depends on what species you are going after. Blue and flathead catfish tend to live in larger lakes and rivers. Channel and bullhead will also be in those large bodies, but they thrive better in smaller environments like small lakes, ponds, and streams. When picking where to cast, you need to analyze a few things:

  • Where is there activity?
  • Where is there cover?
  • Where is the deep water?
To catch catfish, you must first think like a catfish. They spend most of their time in deep, dark water, occasionally surfacing to strike topwater. This means you need to keep an eye out for overhanging branches, divots dug into the side of the bank, and large rock overhangs. Catfish dig holes into banks and will use them as their home. These are excellent spots to drop your bait into.

Other Catfishing Methods

Ocassionally, you will see people catching catfish without bait, and sometimes even without a rod and reel. These are unconventional methods of catfishing.

  • Noodling: When an angler wades into the water in search of a catfish home in the bank. The angler then submerges themself and swims partially into the catfish hole. They then stick their arm inside of the catfish's mouth and muscle it out of its hole. This is a common practice for catching blue and flathead catfish in rivers.
  • Bow and Spear Fishing: This is when an angler is equipped with either a bow and arrow or a long spear. This is an extremely rare method of catfishing and is really only used for one species, Pleco. Pleco catfish are traditionally an aquarium fish that eats the algae off the sides of the tank. They have been released into the wild and absolutely destroy the environments they live in by taking food away from native species. Pleco will not bite any kind of bait so the common practive for them is to spear them. After spearing them, you are required to exterminate the fish, and under no circumstances throw it back.
